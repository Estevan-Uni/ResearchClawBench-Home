Advances in materials databases create an opportunity to uncover descriptors that predict emergent
properties, yet most studies rely on high-throughput ab initio calculations that can diverge from
experiment. Experimentalists instead depend on intuition honed by hands-on work. We present
“Materials Expert-Artiﬁcial Intelligence” (ME-AI), a machine-learning framework that translates this
intuition into quantitative descriptors extracted from curated, measurement-based data. Using a set of
879 square-net compounds described using 12 experimental features, we train a Dirichlet-based
Gaussian-process model with a chemistry-aware kernel. ME-AI reproduces established expert rules
for spotting topological semimetals (TSMs) and reveals hypervalency as a decisive chemical lever in
these systems. Remarkably, a model trained only on square-net TSM data correctly classiﬁes
topological insulators in rocksalt structures, demonstrating transferability. Complementing
electronic-structure theory, our framework scales with growing databases, embeds expert
knowledge, offers interpretable criteria, and guides targeted synthesis, accelerating materials
discovery and rapid experimental validation across diverse chemical families.
Global efforts of the last decade established vast databases of materials1–11,
allowing researchers to query data associated with individual entries. This
coming age of materials data presents an unprecedented opportunity to
discover descriptors of physical properties by inferring and articulating
correlations and trends from the collection of entries. If successful, such
efforts can lead to guided discovery of new materials. Historically, the most
successful of such efforts was that by Mendeleev, who predicted properties of
elemental materials from the empirical trends articulated in the periodic
table. However, discoveries of atomistic features that capture the physical
properties of non-elemental materials remain challenging. The combina-
torial phase space of non-elemental materials and the large dimensionality
of the feature space render the quest out of manual reach.
The increasing availability of machine-learning (ML) tools has enabled
recent developments in ﬁnding stable materials by scaling up ab initio
calculations4,12 and synthesis13,14. However, much of the literature focused on
leveraging ML for computational predictions in the tradition of ab initio
calculations15–21. Left untapped are the intuition and insights that expert
materials growers have honed through many years of experience. We
propose to “bottle” the insights latent in the expert growers’ human intellect
to arrive at descriptors that articulate the insights. Our strategy will be to
supply information and data curated by a materials expert (ME) following
his/her intuition to an ML model, together with expert annotation of the
desired material properties. The mission for the model we dubbed ME-AI
will be to learn the descriptor that can predict the desired properties from
readily available information (see Fig. 1a). By letting ME-AI investigate
expert-curated experimental information, we hope to accelerate discoveries
of empirical laws and allow for the research community to expand upon
explicit articulation of the insight latent to the curated information.
Given the challenging nature of this problem, the likelihood of success
will depend on the reliability of the data and the scope of the search. A
reliable database starts with the ME curating a reﬁned dataset with
experimentally accessible primary features chosen based on intuition from
literature, ab initio calculations, or chemical logic. Regarding the scope of the
search, setting a scope that makes ample use of chemical understanding can
increase the likelihood of success. Based on these criteria, we focus on
learning the descriptor of topological nodal-line semimetals (TSMs) derived
from basic atomistic and structural features, limiting the pool of candidate
materials to square-net structures. Remarkably, we ﬁnd that ME-AI trained
on square-net structures to predict TSMs can also successfully predict
topological insulators among rocksalt structures, exhibiting surprising
generalization ability.

In this work, we introduce ME-AI, a machine-learning framework that
leverages experimentally curated expert intuition to uncover quantitative
descriptors predictive of TSMs. Applying this approach to a dataset of 879
square-net compounds characterized by 12 primary features, ME-AI not
only recovers the known structural descriptor, the “tolerance factor,” but
also identiﬁes four new emergent descriptors. Remarkably, one purely
atomistic descriptor aligns with classical chemical concepts of hypervalency
and the Zintl line. ME-AI demonstrates excellent predictive capabilities and
generalizes beyond square-net compounds to accurately identify topological
insulators within the rocksalt structure family. Our ﬁndings highlight the
potential of combining human expertise with artiﬁcial intelligence to
facilitate targeted materials discovery.
Results
ME-AI Workﬂow
ME-AI aims to reveal the expert intuition (t-factor) and further insights. This
starts with the ME curating a dataset of square-net materials (see Supple-
mentary Methods A). With this curated data, the AI part of ME-AI reveals
the correlations between different primary features (PFs) and discovers the
emergent descriptors composed of primary features using a Gaussian pro-
cess (GP). We choose the PFs to be atomistic or structural, such that we can
interpret the ML results from a chemical perspective. For atomistic features,
wefocuson(a):electronafﬁnity, the change in energy after a neutral atom
gains an electron; (b): (Pauling) electronegativity, the relative ability of an
atom to attract a shared electron pair; and (c): valence electron count, cor-
responding to the number of electrons in a neutral atom. Since each com-
pound consists of different numbers of elements, we built primary atomistic
features in a uniform structure by choosing maximum and minimum values
of these features and the square-net element features (see Fig. 2a). The last
atomistic feature is (d) the estimated face-centered cubic lattice parameter fcc
of the square-net element, which reﬂects the radius of the square-net atom.
Given the prior choice of square-net compounds, two lengths are sufﬁcient
to represent the structural feature variations: (e) the crystallographic char-
acteristic distances dsq and dnn.Intotal,wechose12PFs(seeFig.2a).
Having determined the PFs, we curated an experimentally measured
database of PFs for 879 compounds belonging to the 2D-centered square-
net class in the inorganic crystal structure database (ICSD)31.Thestructure
types of these compounds include PbFCl, ZrSiS (ZrSiTe, AmTe2−x,and
UP2), PrOI, Cu2Sb, ZrCuSiAs-HfCuSi2,LaZn0.5Sb2,PrNiSb2 and CaBe2Ge2.
While we focus on the space group 129, the methodology and features we
have found would work in other space groups, as long as one can deﬁne a
centrosymmetric square net. The expert labeling of the materials is a critical
part of the data curation, where the expert insight is passed on to the model
(see Fig. 1a). The ambitious goal of ME-AI is to go beyond mechanical
scraping of data, but to use the logic routinely employed by materials
experimentalists to give the ML model the opportunity to articulate the
expert’s insight. Our priority in the labeling process was to capture as
comprehensive information as possible. When experimental or computa-
tional band structure is available for a given entry, we label the material
through visual comparison of the band structure to the square-net tight-
binding model band structure in Fig. 1e. This applies to 56% of the entire
database. For alloys, we use the expert chemical logic to label the material
based on the labels of the parent materials. This applies to 38% of the
database. For instance, since HfSiS, HfSiSe, ZrSiS, and ZrSiSe are all TSM, we
expect (Hf,Zr)Si(S,Se) to also be TSM. 6% of the database consists of sto-
chiometric compounds without available band structure information, but
that are closely related to materials with band structure, for instance, by
cation substitution. We again used chemical logic in such cases. Once our
GP model is trained, we inspect the distribution of different labeling
mechanisms in the space of learned emergent features to conﬁrm that
consistent and uniform feature learning occurred across different branches
of the labeling process.
See Supplementary Methods A for details.
From the machine-learning perspective, the task at hand is to use this
relatively small labeled dataset of 12-dimensional PFs to discover effective
descriptors that predict TSMs automatically. The simplest dimensional
reduction approach of principal component analysis (PCA) fails (see Sup-
plementary Discussion A) since PCA cannot incorporate prior knowledge
of the labels. On the other hand, the go-to supervised learning architectures
using neural networks are also inadequate due to the small dataset, lack of
interpretability, and the danger of overﬁtting. ME-AI treats this problem as a
Gaussian Process (GP) classiﬁcation task32 and obtains the descriptors by
learning a purposefully chosen factor analysis matrix parameterizing the
kernel for the GP33. ME-AI achieves a classiﬁcation accuracy above 0.98
(see Supplementary Methods C for hyperparameters for training and sta-
bility testing). While random forest offers a comparable accuracy, it fails to
reveal scientiﬁc insight due to limited interpretability (see Supplementary
Discussion B).
The emergent descriptors
The correlation matrix learned from the GP classiﬁer (see Methods) reveals
the emergent descriptors that identify TSMs. The elements of the
normalized-correlation matrix, {Cmn}[eq:(4)]showninFig.3a reveal the
relative strengths and signs of interaction among all the 72 pairs of PFs. Five
elements of {Cmn} clearly stand out in their magnitudes with varying signs.
We hypothesize that positive (negative) and strong {Cmn} can be effectively
captured by emergent descriptors formed from products (ratios) of PF m
and PF n. To test this hypothesis, we plot the distribution of the products and
ratios, i.e., dsq/dnn, dsq/fcc, χsq/dsq, χsq*dnn,andχsq*fcc,asshowninFig.3b–f.
As shown in Supplementary Discussion C, the distributions conﬁrm small
values of dsq/dnn, dsq/fcc and intermediate values of χsq/dsq, χsq*fcc, and
χsq*dnn as successful descriptors of TSM. These descriptors synthesize
atomistic information of elements forming the material and the structural
information of the compound in three different ways: (1) dsq/dnn relies solely
on structural features, (2) dsq/fcc, χsq*dsq,andχsq*dnn combine structural
and atomistic features, and (3) χsq*fcc is a descriptor solely based on ato-
mistic features. It is noteworthy that ME-AI reproduces t = dsq/dnn as one of
the emergent descriptors without prior knowledge. This bolsters the insight
underlying the t-factor and establishes ME-AI’s capability to “bottle” expert
insight latent to the expert curation of the data.
We now turn to our four newly discovered descriptors. Among these,
the distribution of dsq/fcc (Fig. 3c) is similar to the distribution of the t-factor
(Fig. 3b), although dsq/fcc does a better job in separating TSM from trivial
materials (see Supplementary Discussion C for quantitative analysis of the
distributions). Since fcc estimates the size of the unbonded square-net
atoms, small values of dsq/fcc and that of the t-factor both point to a che-
mically and electronically signiﬁcant role of the square-net planes.
The distributions of the remaining three descriptors, χsq*dnn, χsq/dsq,
and χsq*fcc, are similar in that TSM favors intermediate values of these
descriptors (Fig. 3d, f). This result reveals the underappreciated signiﬁcance
of hypervalency for TSM. The concept of hypervalency accounts for the
seeming violation of the octet rule in molecules and solids, a classic chemical
rule, the main group elements want to reach noble gas conﬁguration with 8
valence electrons to ﬁll the s and p shells34. Hypervalent bonds connect more
than two atoms and allow cases with the seeming violation of the octet rule
to satisfy the octet rule in an extended sense35,36. Hypervalent bonds make
the desired electron count for placing the Fermi level at the right position
possible: it stabilizes half-ﬁlled px and py orbitals by creating covalent bonds
with only one electron per bond. While hypervalent bonds were known to be
possible in square-net materials, a descriptor quantifying hypervalency was
